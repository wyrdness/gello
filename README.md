## Overview

Gello (Greek: Γελλώ; later plural Gelloudes, γελοῦδες) is a female demon attested in Greek literature from the archaic period through the Byzantine and modern Greek folk traditions. Earliest references are in a fragment attributed to Sappho (6th century BC) and in later proverb-collections (Zenobius, 2nd century AD; Hesychius, 5th–6th century), where Gello is said to be the ghost (eidolon) of a young woman who died unwed or childless and returns to harm pregnant women, infants, and virgins. In the Byzantine period the singular figure became a class of beings; John of Damascus (8th century) equated the gelloudes with stryngai (witches who fly at night and strangle children), and Michael Psellos (11th century) wrote a treatise discussing their nature and proposing a connection with the Hebrew Lilith. The 'Legend of Saint Sisinnios' — preserved in Greek, Coptic, Ethiopic, Slavonic, Romanian, and other manuscripts — narrates how the saint pursues Gyllou after she kills the children of Melitene, forcing her to reveal her many secret names so they may be inscribed on protective amulets. The tradition lies behind a vast corpus of Byzantine apotropaic amulets (often invoking Sisinnios or Solomon as a 'holy rider' spearing the demon), exorcisms, and folk practices that remained in use into the 20th century.

## Etymology

- **Origin**: The name Gello has been linked by Carl Frank, Walter Burkert, and M. L. West to the Akkadian/Mesopotamian demon Gallû, a class of underworld spirits associated with sickness and death; this same root has also been argued to underlie the later Arabic/English 'ghoul'. A Greek folk-etymology connected the name to gel- ('grin, laugh').
- **Meaning**: In its earliest Greek attestations, Gello is the unappeased ghost of a young woman dead before her time. The plural gelloudes designates child-killing female demons.
- **First use**: Attested in a fragment of Sappho (6th century BC); the plural gelloudes is first found in John of Damascus (8th century AD).

## Distribution

Originally a localized Greek tradition (attested for Sappho on Lesbos), the Gello / gelloudes complex spread throughout the Greek-speaking Byzantine world and, through the 'Legend of Saint Sisinnios', into Coptic, Ethiopic, Armenian, Romanian, Slavonic, Arabic, Syriac, and Hebrew manuscript traditions across the eastern Mediterranean and Near East.

## Theories & Explanations

### Paranormal / Cryptozoological

**Restless ghost / unappeased dead** _(Sappho, Zenobius, Hesychius of Alexandria)_

Classical interpretation: Gello is the ghost (eidolon) of a young woman who died before her time and unwed, whose bitterness or unfulfilled life makes her dangerous to children and pregnant women.

**Demonic / Satanic witch tradition** _(John of Damascus, Michael Psellos, Leo Allatios)_

Byzantine Christian interpretation: Gello (as the gelloudes) is a class of demons, identified with the stryngai of pagan tradition and ultimately with the Devil's agents; certain women may be accused of becoming gelloudes through diabolical pact.

### Skeptical

**Folkloric explanation for infant mortality** _(Sarah Iles Johnston, Karen Hartnup, Richard P. H. Greenfield)_

Modern scholarship treats Gello as a personification of pre-modern infant mortality, miscarriage, sudden infant death, and puerperal complications, providing a culturally meaningful narrative for losses with otherwise opaque causes.

**Diffusionist / Mesopotamian origin** _(Carl Frank, Walter Burkert, M. L. West)_

The name and figure are argued to derive from the Akkadian Gallû, an underworld demon; Gello is then one node in a wider Mediterranean and Near Eastern reproductive-demon complex.
